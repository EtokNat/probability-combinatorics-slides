Equiprobable events.

Equiprobable — every individual elementary outcome in a sample space has the exact same theoretical chance of happening. Think: a fair coin toss, a single roll of a fair die, a single random draw from a well-shuffled deck of cards.

$$P(\text{Event}) \;=\; \frac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}$$
The condition. This fraction is mathematically invalid unless the denominator consists entirely of perfectly balanced, equiprobable events. If you catch yourself writing a fraction like this where the outcomes are not equally likely, stop — the answer will be wrong.

Rows: score on the red die (1–6)  ·  Columns: score on the blue die (1–6)  ·  Each cell shows the sum, and every cell is equally likely.

  1. Build the sample space. Map every coordinate pair $(a,b)$ from $(1,1)$ to $(6,6)$ into a 2D grid.
  2. Count the total. $6 \times 6 = 36$ equiprobable outcomes.
  3. Count the favourable. Pairs summing to 8: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2) \;\Rightarrow\; 5$ outcomes.
  4. Form the probability.
    $$P(\text{sum}=8) \;=\; \frac{5}{36}$$
The probability is $\dfrac{5}{36} \approx 0.139$.

Reality check. Manual listing of $792$ outcomes inside an exam is a guaranteed failure. You need a counting machine — a formula that produces the length of the list without forcing you to write it.

The counting machine.

Because every selection is equally likely, combinations $^nC_r$ give us the exact length of the list — the count of possibilities — without forcing us to write a single one of them down.

Step 1 · Denominator

Total ways to choose any $5$ balls from the $12$ in the bag.

$$^{12}C_5 \;=\; 792$$
Step 2 · Numerator

Choose $2$ red AND $3$ blue — multiply the two counts.

Red:   $^{5}C_{2} = 10$

Blue:   $^{7}C_{3} = 35$

Favourable $= 10 \times 35 = \mathbf{350}$

$$P(\text{2 red and 3 blue}) \;=\; \frac{^{5}C_{2} \times {}^{7}C_{3}}{^{12}C_{5}} \;=\; \frac{350}{792} \;=\; \frac{175}{396} \;\approx\; 0.442$$
The probability is $\dfrac{175}{396}$.

The formula does not read English.

A bag contains $5$ red balls and $7$ blue balls. Five balls are drawn at random, without replacement. Find the probability that exactly $2$ are red and $3$ are blue.

A committee of $5$ people is to be selected at random from a group consisting of $5$ men and $7$ women. Find the probability that the committee contains exactly $2$ men and $3$ women.

The same mathematical machinery solves both.

$$P \;=\; \frac{^{5}C_{2} \;\times\; {}^{7}C_{3}}{^{12}C_{5}} \;=\; \frac{350}{792} \;=\; \frac{175}{396}$$
Core idea. The $^nC_r$ formula cannot tell the difference between a red marble and a doctor. It does not see “marble” or “teacher” or “student” — it only sees pool size and selection size. Drawing coloured balls is mathematically identical to selecting a human committee.

Two questions that look alike — but need different tools.

The “Handful” — no order.

Question. A bag contains $5$ red balls and $7$ blue balls. Two balls are drawn from the bag at random, without replacement. Find the probability that one ball is red and the other is blue.

Action. Use combinations — order of the handful does not matter.

$$P \;=\; \frac{^{5}C_{1} \times {}^{7}C_{1}}{^{12}C_{2}} \;=\; \frac{5 \times 7}{66} \;=\; \frac{35}{66}$$
$P = \dfrac{35}{66} \approx 0.530$

The “Timeline” — specific order.

Question. From the same bag ($5$ red, $7$ blue), two balls are drawn in sequence, without replacement. Find the probability that the first ball drawn is red and the second ball drawn is blue.

Action. Abandon combinatorics — use sequential fractions. After the first draw, the bag is smaller.

$$P \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{5}{12}}_{\text{1st: red}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{7}{11}}_{\text{2nd: blue}} \;=\; \frac{35}{132}$$
$P = \dfrac{35}{132} \approx 0.265$
Diagnostic question to ask yourself: does the wording specify which object comes first? If yes → Timeline. If the question just asks for a collection of objects → Handful.

Classwork — pick your tier.

Tier 1 · Core translation

Doctors & nurses.

A medical team of $4$ people is to be chosen at random from a group consisting of $6$ doctors and $5$ nurses.

  1. Find the probability that the team chosen contains exactly $2$ doctors and exactly $2$ nurses.

Setup. Denominator is $^{11}C_{4}$; numerator is $^{6}C_{2} \times {}^{5}C_{2}$.

$P = \dfrac{150}{330} = \dfrac{5}{11} \approx 0.455$
Tier 2 · Boundary identification

Green & yellow tokens.

A bag contains $8$ green tokens and $4$ yellow tokens.

  1. (a) Three tokens are drawn from the bag at random, without replacement. Find the probability that exactly $2$ of them are green and $1$ is yellow.
  2. (b) Three tokens are drawn one after the other, without replacement. Find the probability that the first is green, the second is green, and the third is yellow.

Hint. One of these is a Handful; the other is a Timeline. Decide which is which before you start writing.

(a)   $P = \dfrac{112}{220} = \dfrac{28}{55} \approx 0.509$ (b)   $P = \dfrac{8}{12}\cdot\dfrac{7}{11}\cdot\dfrac{4}{10} = \dfrac{28}{165} \approx 0.170$
Tier 3 · Exam synthesis

“At least” cases.

Using the same medical group as Tier 1 ($6$ doctors and $5$ nurses), a team of $4$ people is chosen at random.

  1. Find the probability that the team chosen contains at least $3$ doctors.

Hint. Break the favourable outcome into mutually exclusive cases — exactly $3$ doctors, then exactly $4$ doctors — count each with $^nC_r$, and add the two totals.

$P = \dfrac{100 + 15}{330} = \dfrac{23}{66} \approx 0.348$
Rule for every tier: write the explicit $^nC_r$ setup — or the explicit sequential fractions — before any number comes out of your calculator. Then discuss, then reveal the verified answers.

Assignment — the three Cambridge traps.

Task. Solve these three exam-level constraints. For each one, you must write down your explicit $^nC_r$ setup. Bare decimal answers will receive zero marks.
Question 1 · The VIP

Question. A committee of $5$ people is to be chosen at random from a group of $8$ men and $6$ women. Given that the male Chairman must be included on the committee, find the probability that the committee chosen contains exactly $3$ men.

Hint. If the Chairman takes his seat before the random selection begins, how does your starting pool change, and how many people are you actually choosing at random?

Chairman in $\Rightarrow$ pick $4$ more from $13$ (need $2$ more men & $2$ women):   $P = \dfrac{^{7}C_{2}\cdot{}^{6}C_{2}}{^{13}C_{4}} = \dfrac{315}{715} = \dfrac{63}{143} \approx 0.441$
Question 2 · The Feud

Question. A committee of $4$ people is to be chosen at random from a group of $10$ students. Two of the students, John and Sarah, refuse to work together on the same committee. Find the probability that the committee chosen is acceptable — that is, it does not contain both John and Sarah.

Hint. It is easier to calculate the probability of the disaster happening — both John and Sarah being chosen — and then subtract that probability from $1$.

$P(\text{both J \& S}) = \dfrac{^{8}C_{2}}{^{10}C_{4}} = \dfrac{28}{210} = \dfrac{2}{15}$  $\Rightarrow$  $P(\text{acceptable}) = 1 - \dfrac{2}{15} = \dfrac{13}{15} \approx 0.867$
Question 3 · The Shortcut

Question. A team of $5$ people is to be chosen at random from a group of $6$ doctors and $5$ nurses. Find the probability that the team chosen contains at least $1$ doctor.

Hint. You could calculate exactly $1$, exactly $2$, exactly $3$, exactly $4$ and exactly $5$ doctors — five separate cases. Or you could calculate the probability of a team with zero doctors and subtract that from $1$. Which is safer in an exam?

$P(\text{zero doctors}) = \dfrac{^{5}C_{5}}{^{11}C_{5}} = \dfrac{1}{462}$  $\Rightarrow$  $P(\text{at least }1\text{ doctor}) = 1 - \dfrac{1}{462} = \dfrac{461}{462} \approx 0.998$
