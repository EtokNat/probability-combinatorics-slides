Core Concept · 01

Equiprobable events & the probability fraction.

Definition

Equiprobable — every single elementary outcome has the exact same chance of occurring. Think: a fair coin, a fair die, a random draw from a well-shuffled deck.

$$P(\text{Event}) \;=\; \frac{\text{Favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total outcomes}}$$
When to list by hand

If the total sample space is small (fewer than $\sim\!40$), we map it with a list or a 2D grid. This prevents miscounts — you can literally see every outcome.

The limitation. Choosing a 5-person committee from 20 people gives 15 504 outcomes. No grid will save you. We need a formula.

  1. Build the sample space. One 2D grid, Die A down the side, Die B across the top.
  2. Count the total. $6 \times 6 = 36$ equiprobable outcomes.
  3. Count the favourable. Pairs summing to 8: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2) \;\Rightarrow\; 5$ outcomes.
  4. Form the probability.

Core Concept · 02

The combinatorics bridge.

The problem

Randomly select a 4-student project group from a class of 30. How many possible groups?   27 405.   A 2D grid cannot save us — we would need a grid the size of a wall.

The solution

Use combinations $^nC_r$ to count without listing.

$$^nC_r \;=\; \binom{n}{r} \;=\; \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$$

$n$ = size of the pool  ·  $r$ = how many you pick  ·  order does not matter.

Calculator efficiency

On the Casio ClassWiz, press SHIFT ÷ to access $^nC_r$ directly.

Type $12$, hit the shortcut, type $4$, press =. You get 495 in under two seconds — no factorial by hand.

Why this matters: the exam does not reward factorial arithmetic. It rewards correct selection logic. Free your brain for that.
Worked Example · 02

Calculating the denominator.

Question. A committee of 4 is randomly selected from a group of 7 men and 5 women. How many different committees can be formed?

  1. Identify the pool. $7 + 5 = 12$ total people.
  2. Identify the selection size. We are picking exactly $r = 4$ people from the whole group.
  3. Order matters? No — a committee is a committee regardless of the order you pick its members. So we use combinations, not permutations.
  4. Apply the formula.
    $$^{12}C_4 \;=\; \frac{12!}{4!\,8!} \;=\; 495$$
Total possible committees: 495
Pedagogical note. This $495$ becomes the absolute denominator for any probability question built on this scenario. Hold onto it — we will need it on the very next slide.
Core Concept · 03

Restricting the numerator.

The goal

Count the number of specific, restricted selections that satisfy the condition of the question — these are the favourable outcomes.

The rule of “AND”

When a favourable outcome requires picking from multiple distinct groups (e.g. choose men AND women), count each group separately, then multiply.

$$\text{Numerator} \;=\; \underbrace{^{n_1}C_{r_1}}_{\text{Group 1}} \;\times\; \underbrace{^{n_2}C_{r_2}}_{\text{Group 2}} \;\times\; \cdots$$
Why multiplication? Each way to choose Group 1 can pair with every way to choose Group 2. That is the multiplication principle in one sentence.
Worked Example · 03 · Cambridge exam-style

Putting it all together.

Question. A committee of 4 is selected at random from 7 men and 5 women. Find the probability that the committee contains exactly 2 men and 2 women.

Step 1 · Denominator

Total unrestricted committees of 4 from 12 people.

$$^{12}C_4 \;=\; 495$$
Step 2 · Numerator

Choose 2 men AND 2 women.

Men:  $^{7}C_{2} = 21$

Women:  $^{5}C_{2} = 10$

Favourable $= 21 \times 10 = \mathbf{210}$

$$P(2\text{M and }2\text{W}) \;=\; \frac{210}{495} \;=\; \frac{14}{33} \;\approx\; 0.424$$
$P = \dfrac{14}{33}$

Tier 1 · Core mechanics

Red & blue marbles.

A bag contains 6 red and 4 blue marbles. Three are drawn at random.

  1. How many total ways to draw 3 marbles?
  2. How many ways to draw exactly 2 red and 1 blue?
  3. Find $P(2R \text{ and } 1B)$.
Tier 2 · Exam application

Teachers & students.

A team of 5 is chosen from 4 teachers and 6 students.

  1. Find $P(\text{exactly 1 teacher})$.

Tip. Same group, two separate $^nC_r$ calculations — then multiply.

Tier 3 · High-flyer extension

“At least” cases.

Same group (4 teachers, 6 students). Team of 5.

  1. Find $P(\text{at least 3 teachers})$.

Hint. Break the numerator into mutually exclusive cases — 3 teachers, then 4 teachers — and add.
